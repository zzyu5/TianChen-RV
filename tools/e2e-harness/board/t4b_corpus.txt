The history of computing hardware spans many centuries and involves the gradual development of machines that can perform calculations automatically. Early mechanical calculators, such as the ones designed by Blaise Pascal and Gottfried Leibniz, could add and multiply numbers using systems of gears and wheels. In the nineteenth century, Charles Babbage proposed the Analytical Engine, a general purpose mechanical computer that anticipated many features of modern machines, including a memory unit, an arithmetic unit, and the ability to be programmed with punched cards. Ada Lovelace, who studied Babbage's designs, wrote what is often considered the first computer program and recognized that such a machine could manipulate symbols beyond mere numbers.

The twentieth century brought the transition from mechanical to electronic computing. Vacuum tubes replaced relays, and then transistors replaced vacuum tubes, dramatically reducing the size and power consumption of computers while increasing their speed and reliability. The invention of the integrated circuit allowed thousands and eventually billions of transistors to be placed on a single chip of silicon. This exponential growth in transistor density, often summarized by Moore's law, drove decades of steady improvement in performance and cost. Modern processors combine many cores, deep memory hierarchies, and specialized vector units to accelerate the numerical workloads found in scientific computing, graphics, and machine learning.

Today, open instruction set architectures such as RISC-V invite researchers and companies to design custom processors without licensing restrictions. Vector extensions enable a single instruction to operate on many data elements at once, which is especially valuable for the matrix multiplications at the heart of neural networks. Careful engineering of memory layout, quantization, and kernel scheduling can yield large improvements in throughput on these emerging platforms.
